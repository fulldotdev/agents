# Platform playbook

Read this when direct results are weak, several people share the same name, or the search needs evidence from more than one platform.

## Choose starting points

Start with the smallest set of reliable starting points:

- Record known facts such as name variants, approximate year, role, place, organization, school, event, and handles.
- Use official accounts, staff pages, job pages, team accounts, old team posts, and event pages as organization clues.
- Use supervisors, department heads, known colleagues, former staff, organizers, and public team members as people clues.
- Connect platforms through repeated usernames, the same profile photo, linked websites, matching study or work history, and mutual public connections.

Search the most specific relationship first. A role-specific team page is usually better than a broad city search. When a team has no public account, search through its supervisors, adjacent teams, former employees, and tagged group posts.

## Web and official sources

Use search engines to find direct evidence and source material that social search may hide:

- `"<name>" "<organization>"`
- `"<name>" "<place>" "<role>"`
- `site:linkedin.com/in "<name>" "<place>"`
- `site:instagram.com "<organization>" "<name>"`
- The organization's site plus `team`, `staff`, `work`, `role`, or the relevant year.

Check local news, public PDFs, newsletters, event programs, public school or sports results, archived staff pages, and job descriptions. Use these to confirm spelling, age plausibility, role requirements, season, or local ties. A local mention proves only what it states. It does not prove the person held the target role.

## Instagram

Check these routes:

- Search followers and followed accounts of the official organization for the exact first name and likely variants.
- Check tagged posts, captions, mentions, public team carousels, and adjacent team accounts.
- Use public supervisors and colleagues as starting points when the official account is too broad.
- Search usernames on other platforms. A private Instagram account may have a public LinkedIn, Threads, TikTok, Facebook, or VSCO profile under the same handle.

Do not assume a private account is a dead end. Its username, display name, small photo, mutual follows, and public mentions can still support or weaken a lead. Do not follow it without approval.

Story and highlight views can be visible. Treat opening them as an interaction and ask first. Ordinary public posts and profile pages are read-only unless the platform indicates otherwise.

## LinkedIn

Combine the name with:

- Current and former organization.
- Place, nearby cities, school, industry, and plausible seasonal work.
- Known supervisors or colleagues using connection-of or network filters when available.
- Dates that make the role plausible, including gaps between study, internships, or other jobs.

Search current and former employees of the organization, not only profiles that state the exact target role. Seasonal and informal work is often missing. A candidate appearing in the network of a relevant supervisor or colleague is useful support, but it is not a direct employment match.

## Facebook

Check public pages, old posts, photo tags, comments, staff or team accounts, public friends lists, relevant groups, and events. Role recruitment posts often attract former staff and acquaintances in the comments even when the target never comments.

When practical, count a checked route. "No matching first name among 200 public comments across three role posts" is useful negative evidence. It rules out that route, not the person.

A login may make more search and network filters available to the user. It does not authorize friend requests, group joins, reactions, messages, or access workarounds.

## TikTok, Threads, VSCO, and adjacent platforms

Use these after stronger organization and network routes:

- Reused usernames and display names.
- Public bios, mentions, tagged videos, photo history, and linked accounts.
- Visual confirmation by the user when the identity evidence is already plausible.

Treat these as supporting sources. A matching photo or username without contextual overlap is a weak lead.

## Rank evidence

Use this order when deciding what to investigate next:

1. Direct evidence states the organization, role, event, or relationship in the relevant period.
2. Strong network evidence places the candidate in a role-specific team post or in the public networks of several independent people connected to the context.
3. Context evidence aligns on place, school, work history, age plausibility, season, and likely availability.
4. Identity hints include a name variant, reused handle, current photo, hair, clothing, or shared interests.

Require at least two independent signals before calling someone a strong lead. Keep inferences explicit.

## Feedback checkpoints

Ask questions that remove branches from the search tree:

- "Is the spelling confirmed in your own notes, or could it be a similar name?"
- "Was the role in summer 2025 or 2026? That changes which staff posts matter."
- "Candidates 1 and 3 have the strongest team links. Does either look familiar?"
- "You ruled out 2 because of blond hair. Could that photo be old, or is the hair color decisive?"
- "The remaining route is a story highlight whose owner can see the view. Do you approve opening that exact highlight?"

Avoid vague requests for more information. Show the current evidence and ask for the single detail that best separates the remaining candidates.

## Pivot rules

- If direct name search fails, verify the name from the user's notes, then search role and organization clues.
- If the official account is too broad, move to department heads, team accounts, former staff, and tagged group posts.
- If one platform is private, reuse the handle and context clues on other platforms.
- If appearance eliminates many candidates, retain those with strong network links in case the photo or hairstyle is old.
- If the same candidates repeat, stop broad name searches and add a new relationship, year, organization, or person.
- If only interactive routes remain, state the exact action, what trace it leaves, and ask permission or stop.

## Candidate batches

Keep each round short enough for fast user judgment:

```markdown
1. Candidate A, LinkedIn and Instagram
   Matches: worked in the right place during the right period; connected to a team lead.
   Conflicts: target role is not listed.
   Status: strong.

2. Candidate B, Facebook
   Matches: exact name and a public team photo.
   Conflicts: dates do not align.
   Status: weak.

Question: Does either person look familiar? If not, which detail rules each one out?
```

Update the candidate list from the answer before searching again.
